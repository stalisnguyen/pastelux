A datasheet that says "3000 K, Ra 90" has told you almost nothing. Two products meeting that
description can render a client's marble lobby as warm cream or as slightly sour green, and only
one of them will survive the site walk.

## CCT is a shorthand, not an address

Correlated colour temperature reduces a two-dimensional chromaticity to one number: the
temperature of the blackbody radiator that looks most similar. Any point on a line perpendicular
to the Planckian locus maps to the same CCT.

Which means **two 3000 K sources can sit visibly apart** — one slightly above the locus (green),
one below (pink). The distance is **Duv**, and it is the number that decides whether your white
walls look clean.

- Positive Duv → green/yellow cast. Skin looks ill. Marble looks dirty.
- Negative Duv → pink/magenta cast. Often flattering, sometimes cloying.
- Architectural acceptance window: **|Duv| ≤ 0.006**, tighter for hospitality and healthcare.

Specify CCT **and** Duv, or specify a chromaticity target with an SDCM tolerance. Never CCT alone.

## SDCM: the number that keeps a run of lights looking like one product

MacAdam ellipses describe how far chromaticity can drift before an average observer notices.
Tolerance is quoted in steps.

| Tolerance | Where it belongs |
|---|---|
| 2 SDCM | Museum, high-end retail, visible continuous runs |
| 3 SDCM | Default for any architectural project |
| 5 SDCM | Acceptable only where fittings are never seen together |
| 7 SDCM | Industrial and back-of-house only |

A 5-SDCM run of coves on a white ceiling shows visible banding. This is one of the most common
and most expensive post-completion complaints in the industry, and it is entirely avoidable at
specification stage for a small cost premium.

## CRI, and its blind spot

Ra averages the colour shift of eight **pastel** test samples, R1–R8. Nothing saturated is in
that average. A phosphor-converted LED can be optimised to score well on eight desaturated
patches while rendering deep red as brown.

That is why **R9** — strong red — is the number to demand alongside Ra:

- **R9 ≥ 50**: minimum for anything with people in it
- **R9 ≥ 90**: retail food, healthcare, hospitality, timber and textile-heavy interiors

Skin tone, raw meat, red wine, teak, brick and every warm textile in the building depend on R9.
It is routinely the weakest figure on a datasheet, and it is routinely left off the spec.

## TM-30: two numbers instead of one

IES TM-30 uses 99 real-world colour samples and reports two figures:

- **Rf — fidelity.** How accurately colours match the reference. Analogous to Ra but far more
  robust. Target **Rf ≥ 85**.
- **Rg — gamut.** Whether the source saturates or mutes colour overall. 100 is neutral,
  above 100 saturates, below 100 mutes. Target **Rg 95–110** for general architectural work.

The pair matters because they are independent. A source can have Rf 92 and Rg 88 — accurate but
visibly flat and lifeless. Retail often deliberately wants Rg 105–115 to make merchandise pop.
Rf alone cannot express that intent; Ra cannot express it at all.

TM-30 also produces a colour vector graphic showing *which* hue bins are shifted and in which
direction. When a client says "the reds look wrong", that graphic is how you find out whether
they are right.

## A specification that actually protects you

> Nominal 3000 K, Duv 0.000 ± 0.004, ≤3 SDCM initial and maintained,
> Ra ≥ 90, R9 ≥ 50, TM-30 Rf ≥ 85 and Rg 95–110.
> Chromaticity maintenance ≤ 3 SDCM over the warranty period.
> LM-80 data with TM-21 projection at the specified drive current and Tc to be provided.

Two clauses in there do most of the work and are almost always missing. **"Maintained"** SDCM
stops a supplier delivering a tight initial bin that drifts apart within two years. And
**LM-80 data at the actual drive current and case temperature** stops an L70 figure measured on
a cool bench being applied to a fitting sealed in an insulated ceiling void at 65 °C.

## The practical check

Before you commit to a product across a whole building, get three samples from **different
production batches**, run them side by side on a white wall for an hour, and look at them next to
the actual finish samples. Every number above is a proxy for that fifteen-minute test.
